         SOTONSHIP 2026 · TRACK BRIEF

  Responsible AI

       Friday 16 ­ Sunday 18 October 2026 · Barclays Eagle Labs Southampton

The challenge

Build a product that makes AI useful and trustworthy for a specific person, and pitch it as a business. You
can create a tool that helps others develop, test or govern AI, or an AI-powered product in any sector where
responsible design is central to the value it delivers. There is no fixed problem, sector or technology: you
choose what to build and show why it matters.
RAi UK brings together technical and wider social perspectives on responsible AI, with research spanning
sectors including health, law enforcement and financial services. [1] Your challenge is to turn a specific need
into a working product, with evidence of benefit, a clear buyer and a credible way to grow.
SotonShip is about shipping. A focused product that one customer could trial after the weekend is stronger
than a broad promise to make all AI safe. Responsibility should change what your product does, how it is
tested or what its users can control.
Before you start building, describe your project in one sentence:

   We help [a specific person] to [do or decide something] using [AI or an AI assurance tool],
   while reducing [a specific risk to people].

For example: We help customer-support teams test whether their AI answers are supported by approved
sources before a chatbot update reaches customers.

Who you build for and what fits the track

Choose one main user and one potential buyer; they may be different people. Your user might be a developer,
a service worker, a teacher, an AI procurement team, a small business or someone affected by an AI
decision. Design around one workflow and identify who benefits, who could be harmed and who can put
mistakes right.
Any sector fits. Possible themes include fairness, privacy, security, reliability, transparency, accessibility,
human oversight, participation and environmental impact. You do not have to address every theme: choose
the risks that matter for your user and make the improvement testable.
The following do not fit, because they have no clear product or cannot be tested responsibly in a weekend:

   a generic chatbot, ethics checklist or dashboard with no specific user, decision or action;
   claims that a system is "fair", "safe" or "compliant" without a defined scope and supporting evidence;

What every project must show

A real problem and people affected. Say who has the problem, how often it occurs and what they do today.
Support it with research, published evidence or a conversation with someone affected. Explain a concrete
benefit or harm, not just a general concern about AI.

AI at the core. Show the AI capability your product uses or improves. An assurance tool can qualify by
testing or governing a running AI system; it does not need an LLM added to its checker. Compare the
important step against a simple baseline, such as rules, manual review or the same model without your
safeguard.

A product that works. Let a user complete the main task from start to finish. Use public or openly licensed
material and reproducible test cases, with sources and dates visible. Label synthetic inputs clearly. Keep test
cases separate from examples used to tune the system, and report sample sizes as well as results.

A business case. Pitch a business, not only a research idea: identify the user, paying customer or funder,
problem cost, proposed price or funding model, and route to a first pilot. Estimate AI, hosting and human-
review costs per use. Explain why a buyer would choose you over existing tools and what changes for a
second customer.

Evidence of responsibility and honest limits. Show one measurable improvement and its trade-off, such
as fewer unsupported answers at the cost of more handoffs. Demonstrate one failure and what happens
next. State who can review, override or challenge the output, and what evidence is still needed before real
deployment.

Directions we want to see

These are optional, research-informed starting points. Build one, combine them or ignore them completely.
Original ideas are judged on the same terms. The papers support the underlying methods or concerns; the
products, buyers and business models below are proposals to validate, not proven commercial outcomes.

   A release check for customer-support AI.

   RAGAs proposes ways to evaluate retrieval-augmented answers, including their faithfulness to
   retrieved material. [2] Build a tool that runs test questions against a support chatbot, highlights
   unsupported claims and creates a review queue. A software company or support agency could pay per
   bot or test run. Start with licensed public help pages and deliberately answerable and unanswerable
   questions. Compare against the same bot without the check; measure missed errors and false alarms
   against human-labelled answers. Automated evaluation is evidence for a reviewer, not proof that every
   answer is true.

A fairness check before an AI update.

Gender Shades found substantial differences in error rates across gender and skin-type groups in three
commercial gender classifiers. [3] That motivates checking performance across relevant groups, rather
than relying on one average; it does not establish the same disparities in every domain. Build a release-
review tool for one classifier, such as routing customer requests. A product team could buy a
subscription covering its models. Use labelled synthetic cases or a suitably licensed benchmark,
compare versions and show subgroup error rates, sample sizes and trade-offs. Do not infer sensitive
traits from names or faces. Synthetic results demonstrate the workflow, not fairness in a real
population.

An evidence assistant for buying AI.

Model Cards and Datasheets for Datasets propose structured documentation of intended uses,
evaluations and data provenance. [4] [5] Build an assistant that compares a buyer's needs with
supplier documentation, quotes supporting passages and flags missing evidence for a person to
review. A small company, procurement team or consultancy could pay per supplier assessment. Test on
public model cards plus clearly labelled fictional supplier packs with planted omissions. Compare
against a manual checklist; measure missed gaps, invented claims and review time. Sell faster
evidence review, not automatic certification or legal approval.

A service tool that knows when to hand over.

SelectiveNet studies prediction with a reject option and the trade-off between error and coverage. [6]
Apply that principle to a narrow service workflow: an AI tool routes maintenance requests when
evidence is sufficient and sends uncertain cases to a worker. A facilities provider could pay per site or
request. Use synthetic requests with known categories, including ambiguous and unfamiliar examples.
Compare with routing every request automatically; report errors, the fraction handled automatically and
the human queue. This is an adaptation to test, not a claim that the paper validates your workflow.
Show what happens when the reviewer is unavailable.

Track rules

The event-wide rules apply: teams of four, hacking runs from Friday 20:00 to Sunday 13:00, and you own
what you build. In addition, for this track:

   Build your entry during the event and create its repository after hacking opens. Open-source libraries,
   templates and pre-trained models are fine, but your own earlier projects are not.
   Credit research, code, models and data, with dates and licences. Disclose AI-generated work, prior
   components and outside help in your README.
   Label cached and synthetic data. Show uncertainty and avoid safety, fairness or compliance claims
   beyond what your tests support.
   Do not use private customer records or sensitive personal data in the demo, and do not send confidential
   information to external AI services. Test only systems you own or are authorised to test.
   Use a sandbox, with no real consequential decisions about people. For health, safety, legal or financial
   uses, show qualified human review and link to official guidance.

Deliverables and pitch

Each team submits three things on its team page on the SotonShip Live website, before submissions lock at
13:00 on Sunday:

   A demo video of 1 to 3 minutes showing your product in use. Every team must upload its video to
   YouTube and submit the link.
   A short summary of about 150 words that explains who your user is, what problem they have and why
   it matters, what your solution does and how it could scale.
   A link to your GitHub repository.

On Sunday afternoon you then pitch in VC for a Day. The Judging & how to win page covers the submission
requirements and the suggested pitch structure in full.
